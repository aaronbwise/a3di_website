The problem
Every survey feeding the global child-malnutrition estimates needs a quality check. With 1,000+ in the database and more each round, manual expert review can’t keep pace — or stay consistent between reviewers.
What I built
A configuration-driven pipeline computing 136 quality indicators per survey, scored through 5 independent statistical channels into a transparent, tiered review queue — evidence, not a black-box score.

01 Challenge

Manual review doesn’t scale to a global database

The UNICEF/WHO/World Bank Joint Malnutrition Estimates maintain the global database of anthropometric surveys used to track child nutrition worldwide. Before a survey enters the published estimates, it must pass quality review — but with 1,000+ surveys and new rounds arriving constantly, hand review breaks down.

The existing process leaned on ad-hoc checks and expert judgement, making consistency hard to hold across reviewers and across years. Subtle issues — digit preference in measurements, age heaping, implausible z-score distributions, internally incoherent prevalence — are easy to miss without systematic screening.

The real requirement

An automated first pass that flags surveys with transparent evidence a human can act on — not a binary accept/reject machine that reviewers would never trust.

02 Approach

One survey in, 136 indicators, 5 independent verdicts

The pipeline computes 136 quality indicators per survey across 10 dimensions, then scores each survey through 5 independent statistical channels — each answering a different question about quality, so a flag always comes with a reason.

Why it earns a statistician’s trust

Channels A–C judge how data was collected; E–F judge whether the resulting estimates are plausible — a survey can have clean measurements but implausible prevalence, or vice versa. The mixed-effects models nest by WHO × World Bank region (17 groups) with per-country random intercepts, so single-survey countries borrow strength from their region while long-history countries are judged against their own trajectory. Fitted parameters persist as Parquet — language-agnostic across Python, R, and Stata.

The split means reviewers ignore 70% of the database with confidence and concentrate on the 4% that three or more methods independently distrust. Because the rejected-survey validation was scored out-of-sample, the lift over baseline shows the system is tracking real quality, not memorising the training set.

The takeaway

Automated screening only earns trust when it hands reviewers structured evidence — not a verdict. Five methods, five reasons, one queue.
